# Grounding an Agentic Autonomous Cyber SOC in Real Analyst Workflows — UX Research Dossier (Decode SIH 2026, Track 3 "Bharat Pragati" PS4)

## Executive Summary (10 bullets)
- SOC work is dominated by manual toil and noise: 64% of analysts spend over half their time on tedious manual work and 66% believe half-to-all of their tasks could be automated (Tines Voice of the SOC, 468 analysts at 500+ employee firms); 66% of SOC teams cannot keep pace with alert volume (SANS 2024). This is the core UX problem to solve. [UX-F01, UX-F09]
- Analysts drown in alerts: enterprises face ~960 alerts/day on average and >3,000/day at 20k+ employee firms, generated by an average of 28 tools; Prophet Security's survey of 282 leaders found the average alert waits 56 minutes before anyone looks at it and takes ~70 minutes to investigate fully, with 62% ignored entirely; peer-reviewed figures cite 3,832 alerts/day with 62% ignored. [UX-F07, UX-F08]
- Tool sprawl is the hidden tax: the average SOC runs dozens of tools; a single investigation is a "tab marathon" across ~5 consoles, and analysts lose context on every switch. Context-gathering, not analysis, consumes most analyst time. [UX-F10, UX-F11]
- Burnout is severe and quantified: 71% report burnout (Tines), 72% rate SOC pain ≥7/10 and 71% are likely to quit (Devo), and 70% of analysts with ≤5 years experience leave within 3 years (SANS 2024). Retention is a business case for automation. [UX-F12, UX-F13]
- Distrust of AI is specific, not generic: only 11% of leaders are comfortable with AI acting autonomously without review (Kyndryl/Splunk); the top demand is transparency — 90% say explainable AI decisions are critical (Torq 2026, 450+ CISOs/SOC leaders). [UX-F16, UX-F18]
- Peer-reviewed HCI research is decisive: analysts accept AI verdicts even at lower accuracy IF explanations are evidence-backed and role-appropriate (arXiv CORTEX, N=248 survey + 24 interviews). Design for trust *calibration*, not trust maximization. [UX-F20, UX-F21]
- The winning interaction pattern across market leaders (Copilot, Charlotte, Purple AI, XSIAM, Dropzone, Prophet, Torq) is consistent: an evidence-first investigation narrative + visible chain-of-reasoning + confidence + one-click approve/deny + immutable audit trail + feedback learning. [UX-F24–UX-F31]
- Autonomy must be tiered and policy-guarded: leaders converge on "human-in-the-loop for impactful actions, human-on-the-loop for routine," with approval gates flagged per action-risk tier (Palo Alto XSIAM, Torq). [UX-F27, UX-F28]
- India is a global SOC delivery hub under a uniquely harsh clock: CERT-In mandates 6-hour incident reporting (2022) — the world's strictest — stacked with DPDP Act's 72-hour breach notice; this makes fast, auto-drafted, compliant reporting a killer India-specific feature. [UX-F34, UX-F41, UX-F42]
- India context sharpens the pitch: 22.68 lakh cybercrime complaints (+42% YoY) and ₹22,845.73 crore lost in 2024 (MoS Home Affairs, Lok Sabha); a ~1M+ talent shortfall; Indian IT-services SOC salaries of ₹3.5–6 LPA at L1 with high Tier-1 attrition — an autonomous Tier-1 is directly relevant to TCS/Wipro/Verizon judges. [UX-F37, UX-F38, UX-F39]

## 1. A Day in the Life: SOC Analyst Workflows, Tooling, and Where Minutes Are Lost

**Tiered structure (baseline, corroborated by peer-reviewed and vendor sources).** The universal SOC model has three tiers. Tier-1 (Triage): alert validation, severity assessment, escalation. Tier-2 (Incident Response): investigation, event correlation, attribution. Tier-3 (Threat Hunting): proactive hunting, forensics, detection-rule refinement. This mapping is stated verbatim in the arXiv CORTEX study (Rastogi et al., 2025) and matches Radiant Security and Indian MSSP career descriptions. [UX-F02] (Confidence: High — consistent across peer-reviewed + vendor + India job-market sources.)

**Tier-1 workflow.** Tier-1 monitors dashboards, reviews alert queues, and performs initial triage: checking severity, context, and impact; most alerts are filtered by predefined rules and confidence scores; benign alerts are closed/suppressed; genuine ones are escalated to Tier-2 with notes and artifacts attached in the case-management system. Indian practitioner guides describe an L1 analyst writing "50+ tickets per shift." [UX-F03] (Confidence: High.)

**Tier-2/Tier-3 workflow.** Tier-2 performs deep-dive investigations using timeline views, process trees, and threat-intelligence data to determine root cause and scope; Tier-3 hunts proactively and refines detection rules. [UX-F04] (Confidence: High.)

**Tools touched per incident.** A modern investigation spans SIEM (Splunk, QRadar, Microsoft Sentinel, Elastic), EDR (CrowdStrike, SentinelOne, Microsoft Defender), identity (Okta, Active Directory), cloud consoles (AWS/GCP/Azure), threat-intel portals, ticketing/case management, and chat (Slack/Teams). Dropzone lists 40+ native integration targets across exactly these categories. [UX-F05] (Confidence: High.)

**Context-switching per incident — the "tab marathon."** ManageEngine's practitioner account describes a single login-anomaly investigation as "five tools, 15 minutes, and a fractured mental model of the threat," with the analyst losing context on every switch (timestamp in one tool, process data in another, network traffic in a third) — giving the attacker "a 15-minute head start." A separate example cites "six minutes just confirming a brute-force attempt, cross-referencing SIEM logs, EDR failures, and a WHOIS lookup across three different tools." [UX-F10] (Confidence: High — practitioner/vendor blog, but concretely consistent with multiple independent sources.)

**Tool sprawl per shift.** D3 Security states "the average SOC manages 83 security tools from nearly 30 vendors"; Vectra AI (2026) reports 69% of organizations use more than 10 detection-and-response tools; the AI SOC Market Landscape 2025 report cites an average of 28 different tools generating alerts. [UX-F11] (Confidence: Med — vendor figures; the "83 tools" is [1-SRC][VENDOR] but directionally corroborated.)

**Where minutes are lost.** (a) Context-gathering dominates: Devo's research (via DeepTempo) found "most analysts unknowingly investigate the same incidents multiple times because different tools present the same underlying activity differently," and that alerts arrive with minimal context forcing manual pivoting. [UX-F06] (b) False-positive investigation: a Trend Micro survey found analysts spend 25–27% of their time handling false positives (cited in ACM Computing Surveys 2025). [UX-F14] (c) Documentation/reporting: analysts would reclaim automated time to "update operational documentation" (Tines) — reporting is a recognized time sink. [UX-F01] (d) Latency to first look: Prophet Security's survey of 282 leaders found the average alert waits 56 minutes before anyone even looks at it and takes ~70 minutes to investigate completely. [UX-F08]

**MTTD/MTTR benchmarks.** SANS 2024 Detection & Response Survey: 67% of orgs track MTTR, 52% track MTTD. SANS 2023 IR Survey: top-quartile orgs detect within 60 minutes; >half within 5 hours. IBM Cost of a Data Breach 2025: 158 days to identify a breach plus 83 to contain = a 241-day mean lifecycle, "the lowest it's been in nine years"; the global average breach cost fell 9% to USD 4.44M — reflecting stealthy intrusions vs. real-time alerts. Dropzone reports traditional Mean-Time-to-Conclusion of 30–40 minutes per alert vs. 3–11 minutes AI-augmented. [UX-F15] (Confidence: High for SANS/IBM; the Dropzone MTTC figures are [VENDOR].)

**Shift handover and its failure modes.** 24/7 SOCs pass context between shifts, and handoff failures are a documented, high-cost weakness. A cybersecurity-specific arXiv study ("Passing the Baton," 2026) notes that many cyber-incident-handling errors occur during handoffs, drawing on healthcare where 80% of major errors were attributed to miscommunication during handovers. Practitioner sources identify four canonical handoff failure modes: the "check in 30 minutes" handoff (no investigative context — incoming analyst restarts from scratch), the "hypothesis-as-conclusion" handoff (conclusion without supporting evidence), the "verbatim log dump" (800 lines of raw evidence, no interpretation), and "missing time sensitivity" (an expiring correlation rule treated like a low-urgency item). Common failure: verbal-only handover → details forgotten, context lost. [UX-F32, UX-F33] (Confidence: High — arXiv + multiple practitioner templates converge.)

## 2. What Analysts Hate and What They Distrust About AI/Autonomy

**Burnout and attrition (quantified).** Tines Voice of the SOC: 71% experiencing some level of burnout; 55% likely to switch jobs within a year (the 2022 edition found 64% likely to switch). Devo 2021 SOC Performance Report: 72% rate SOC-analyst pain ≥7/10; Devo 2019/2020: 65–78% say working in the SOC is "very painful" and 65% have considered quitting; Devo 2022: 71% likely to quit, 78% work overtime (avg 7 hrs/week), average time to fill a SOC role is 7 months. SANS 2024: 70% of analysts with ≤5 years experience leave within 3 years. [UX-F12, UX-F13] (Confidence: High — multiple independent surveys.)

**What analysts hate about current tooling.** (a) Manual toil: 64% spend >half their time on tedious manual work; 66% believe half-to-all of their tasks could be automated (Tines). [UX-F01] (b) Tool sprawl / poor integration: Devo 2022 found one-third of analysts struggle operating across too many tools; Splunk State of Security 2025 found 46% spend more time maintaining tools than defending, and 78% report dispersed security tools. [UX-F17] (c) Alert noise: the Osterman Research 2024 report (sponsored by Dropzone, cited by NIST) found almost 90% of SOCs are overwhelmed by backlogs and false positives, with more than 80% of analysts reporting they feel constantly behind; false-positive rates frequently exceed 50%, some report 80%. [UX-F09, UX-F19]

**Alert fatigue as a safety failure.** The desensitization risk is concrete: after dismissing thousands of false alarms, cognitive fatigue makes analysts miss the real one — a dynamic linked publicly to the 3CX breach. Peer-reviewed data (ACM Computing Surveys 2025) cites Trend Micro findings that 54% of SOC teams feel overwhelmed by alerts and 55% lack confidence in prioritizing/responding. [UX-F14] (Confidence: High.)

**What analysts distrust about AI — specifics.** (a) Autonomy discomfort: only 11% of respondents are comfortable with AI acting autonomously without review (Kyndryl, via CIO Dive); only 11% fully trust AI for mission-critical tasks (Splunk State of Security 2025). [UX-F16] (b) Black-box verdicts: the dominant fear is "a black box quietly making a bad call at 2 a.m." (UnderDefense). Torq's 2026 AI SOC Leadership Report (Sapio Research survey of 450+ CISOs/SOC leaders across four countries) found the top concerns were NOT job-replacement or autonomy fears but fundamental visibility/control needs; 90% say explainable AI decisions are critical to a true AI SOC platform, 46% say the ability to see how AI reaches its conclusions would be the single biggest confidence booster, and 92% say something is actively reducing their trust in AI. [UX-F18] (c) The adoption-confidence gap: 97% expressed confidence in AI for triage but only 37% actually applied AI to triage — the gap is driven by trust (Torq 2026). [UX-F22] (d) Academic corroboration: 65% of analysts express skepticism toward AI alerts and 79% favor hybrid human–AI models over full automation (ACM Digital Threats 2025, N=19 interviews + vendor analysis). [UX-F23] (Confidence: High — multiple survey + peer-reviewed sources.)

**Fear of destructive automated actions.** The specific fear is auto-remediation acting wrongly: "If AI disables an account or quarantines a host, the team needs to know why" (Torq). This is why every serious vendor gates impactful/destructive actions behind human approval (see Section 3). [UX-F26] (Confidence: High.)

## 3. Human–AI Interaction Patterns That Work

Studying the market leaders and peer-reviewed HCI research yields a convergent set of UX patterns.

**Evidence-first investigation narrative.** The strongest pattern is presenting a written, junior-analyst-style narrative: hypothesis → what was checked → what was ruled out → recommendation, with a clean evidence package. Dropzone's reports "read like a junior analyst wrote them"; Intezer emphasizes "complete transparency"; D3 Morpheus "writes a coherent threat narrative every analyst can read" and maps to MITRE ATT&CK. Microsoft Security Copilot "synthesizes into clear, risk-relevant findings with supporting evidence trails." [UX-F24] (Confidence: High — consistent across ≥4 products.)

**Chain-of-reasoning display + provenance.** Torq documents "every agentic action in a transparent timeline view that maps the reasoning leading to each execution," with planning/reasoning/execution reviewable in real time. Prophet Security exposes a per-alert investigation plan, queries, and evidence for review (rather than static playbooks). The academic recommendation (arXiv CORTEX) is that analysts strongly prefer contextual depth and rationale over "mere presentation of outcomes on dashboards." [UX-F25] (Confidence: High.)

**Confidence scoring — displayed honestly, not as a clean single number.** Independent commentary warns against a single "clean accuracy percentage" that hides where pain lives; best practice shows detection rate, false-positive rate, and absolute alert counts together. Academic guidance: include counter-evidence ("80% likelihood of threat, but no prior lateral movement detected") and highlight model blind spots ("training data did not include this device type"). [UX-F30] (Confidence: High — peer-reviewed + practitioner.)

**One-click approve/deny of proposed actions, gated by risk.** Palo Alto XSIAM's Autonomous Playbooks add a dedicated "Potential Response" section highlighting impactful commands "complete with a flag for any actions that require manual user approval, or where certain assets might be excluded by an exclusion policy." Run supervised across 50 enterprise customers since January 2026, analysts approved the XSIAM analyst agent's recommended actions 94% of the time — and in the 6% of disagreements, post-incident analysis showed the agent would have been correct in roughly half. Torq executes routine response autonomously but flags sensitive actions for human-in-the-loop approval with manual override always available. [UX-F27] (Confidence: High.)

**Human-in-the-loop vs human-on-the-loop autonomy tiers.** The market has converged on configurable autonomy: Prophet offers three modes (fully automated resolution / analyst-in-the-loop confirmation / automatic escalation). Dropzone can auto-close benign alerts (more autonomous); Prophet defaults conservative (recommend, human acts). Torq runs "human-on-the-loop by design" with >90% of cases remediated autonomously but critical actions surfaced to humans. The design principle: autonomy scales with (trust × routineness) and is constrained by (risk × complexity) — formalized in the arXiv "Trusted Autonomy" framework (2025). [UX-F28] (Confidence: High.)

**Immutable audit trails / explainability views.** Torq keeps "immutable audit logs — every AI decision, action, and reasoning chain recorded and uneditable," answering the CISO question "What did the AI do, and why?" Palo Alto Cortex AgentiX ships "full auditability" with role-based access controls. Academic guidance adds: log which explanations were viewed before an action ("analyst viewed counterfactual before approving suppression") as evidence of due diligence. [UX-F29] (Confidence: High.)

**Feedback loops where analyst corrections retrain the system.** Torq Reflex "continuously trains dedicated AI models based on a SOC team's confirmed verdicts and corrections," matching analyst-corrected verdicts 85% of the time immediately. Dropzone uses "context memory" that gets richer as the team gives feedback; Intezer feeds investigation outcomes into detection engineering as a closed loop. Prophet "learns from analyst feedback over time." [UX-F31] (Confidence: High.)

**Peer-reviewed HCI findings (decisive for the UX criterion).**
- *Evidence beats accuracy for trust:* CORTEX (arXiv 2503.02065, N=248 survey + 24 interviews) found analysts "were consistently willing to accept XAI outputs, even in cases of lower predictive accuracy, when explanations were perceived as relevant and evidence-backed," and preferred contextual depth over dashboard outcomes. [UX-F20]
- *Role-aware explanation depth:* the same study's "Trust–Explainability curve" shows trust rises with moderate detail then declines under complexity overload; Tier-1 needs concise "why it's likely malicious," Tier-3 needs deep model rationale. A context-agnostic explanation causes miscalibrated trust (Tier-1 blind faith; Tier-3 dismissal). [UX-F21]
- *Explanations can backfire (automation bias):* multiple HCI sources warn that the mere presence of a fluent explanation is read as a "signal of competence" and can increase over-reliance even when the AI is wrong; true calibration needs friction/forcing functions ("Why might this recommendation be wrong?"). Design for trust *calibration*, not trust *maximization*. [UX-F30, UX-F43]
- *Calibrated trust framework:* over-reliance (automation bias) and under-reliance (algorithm aversion) are both failure modes; the goal is "appropriate reliance" via transparency, uncertainty communication, explainability, and human-in-the-loop controls (Springer IJIS 2026; arXiv frameworks). [UX-F44]

## 4. India-Specific Realities

**India as a global SOC delivery hub.** TCS, Wipro, HCLTech, Infosys, and Tech Mahindra operate globally distributed 24×7 Cyber Defense/Fusion Centers serving F500/G2000 clients under follow-the-sun models. HCLTech was named a Leader in the 2023 Gartner Magic Quadrant for Managed Security Services and runs six Cybersecurity Fusion Centers; Wipro operates multiple Cyber Defense Centers (Bengaluru/Hyderabad) with its Holmes AI platform; Infosys runs the Cyber Next platform; these are the exact firms whose engineers/PMs judge the finale. [UX-F35] (Confidence: High for structure; specific per-firm platform claims are [VENDOR]/press.)

**Staffing structure and skill levels.** Indian MSSPs (TCS, Wipro, Infosys, Paladion, NetEnrich, plus specialists Inspira, Sequretek, Eventus) hire L1/L2/L3 analysts in quarterly batches. L1 is entry-level (CompTIA Security+ / basic networking); most MSSPs run L1 on 24×7 rotations; by L2 analysts typically move to daytime/hybrid shifts. Wipro's India SOC posting confirms L2 "handling incidents escalated by the L1 team in 24×7 rotational shifts." [UX-F36] (Confidence: High.)

**Talent shortage (India and global).** NASSCOM has cited a shortage of ~1 million cybersecurity professionals in India; ISC2-framed analysis suggests India needs ~3 million professionals against far fewer trained practitioners. Globally, the ISC2 2024 Cybersecurity Workforce Study (n=15,852, Sept 2024) found a gap of exactly 4,763,963 professionals — a 19.1% YoY rise — with the active workforce flat at 5.5M (+0.1%) against 10.2M needed. DSCI–SANS Indian Cyber Security Skilling Landscape Report (2026): 35% of organizations report significant difficulty recruiting. [UX-F37] (Confidence: Med for India figures — sources vary; High for the ISC2 global figure.)

**Salaries, attrition, night shifts.** Indian IT-services (TCS/Infosys/Wipro/HCL) pay fresher SOC analysts ~₹3.5–5 LPA; GCCs/product firms pay 30–40% more (₹5.5–8 LPA); by year 3 an L2 reaches ₹8–12 LPA. Glassdoor pegs Wipro SOC analyst average ~₹4.15–4.2 LPA. A "shift premium" applies — night shifts add ₹1.5–3 LPA in some postings; TCS pays a night-shift allowance of ₹360/shift (9 PM–7 AM, ≥4.5 hrs) plus transport with women-safety transit provisions. India IT-services attrition ran ~13% LTM in FY2025 (ICRA), down from a ~23% peak in FY2022; India SOC-specific attrition is NOT FOUND as a discrete figure, but practitioner guidance urges leaving Tier-1 within 18–24 months. [UX-F38, UX-F39] (Confidence: High for salary bands/shift allowance; Med for attrition — proxy figure.)

**English vs regional-language.** Security-specific language-barrier evidence is NOT FOUND. Qualitatively, Indian SOC job postings routinely require English + strong communication (driven by serving US/European clients in follow-the-sun models), e.g., AT&T's Hyderabad SOC posting stresses documenting/presenting incident findings. This supports a UX inference: multilingual/plain-language report generation lowers the cognitive and communication barrier for tier-2/tier-3-city analysts. [UX-F40] (Confidence: Med — qualitative job-board evidence; the design implication is [INFERENCE].)

**Alert volume per analyst (India).** No India-specific per-analyst figure is published. Global benchmarks (applicable directionally): ~174 alerts/analyst/day (UnderDefense); ~960–4,400 alerts/day per SOC; 3,832/day with 62% ignored (peer-reviewed). Wipro's specific "15 billion events/day" claim is NOT VERIFIED from a primary source and should not be cited. [UX-F08] (Confidence: Med — global proxy; India-specific NOT FOUND.)

**CERT-In 2022 Directions — the 6-hour mandate (flagship India regulation).** CERT-In's Directions of 28 April 2022 (No. 20(3)/2022, under §70B IT Act), effective 27–28 June 2022, require service providers, intermediaries, data centres, body corporates, and government organisations to report specified cyber incidents (Annexure I) to CERT-In within **6 hours** of noticing or being made aware — described as the world's strictest reporting timeline (vs GDPR's 72h, US CIRCIA's 72h). Non-compliance invites action under §70B(7): imprisonment up to 1 year and/or fine up to ₹1 lakh. Operational impact: the clock is tied to *awareness*, not root-cause completion; practitioner guidance recommends a detection-to-escalation target under 2 hours; the mandate stresses nights/weekends and cross-team coordination — precisely the windows an autonomous SOC covers. Additional obligations: 180-day log retention and NTP synchronization. [UX-F34, UX-F41] (Confidence: High — primary CERT-In PDF + legal analyses.)

**DPDP Act 2023 — 72-hour breach notification.** Under §8(6) DPDP Act 2023 and Rule 7 of the DPDP Rules 2025 (notified 13 Nov 2025), Data Fiduciaries must notify the Data Protection Board of India "without delay" and file a detailed report within 72 hours, plus notify every affected Data Principal — no materiality threshold. Penalties up to ₹200 crore (failure to notify) and ₹250 crore (inadequate safeguards). A single breach can trigger BOTH the CERT-In 6-hour and DPDP 72-hour clocks simultaneously; BFSI adds RBI/SEBI/IRDAI sector reporting (often 2–6 hours). [UX-F42] (Confidence: High.)

**India cybercrime scale (impact framing).** As stated by the Minister of State for Home Affairs (Bandi Sanjay Kumar) in the Lok Sabha: 22.68 lakh cybercrime incidents were logged on the NCRP in 2024 (+42.08% YoY), with citizens losing ₹22,845.73 crore to cyber criminals (up from ₹7,465.18 crore in 2023 — a 206% rise); NCRP averaged ~50,000 complaints/day; ~85% of complaints are financial fraud. I4C's CFCFRMS prevented/blocked ₹5,489 crore in 2024. [UX-F45] (Confidence: High — government data via quality press; some outlets vary on complaint counts, report the MHA figure.)

## 5. Output Synthesis

### Ten UX design principles for an agentic SOC product (each traced to evidence)

1. **Lead with an evidence-first narrative, not a verdict.** Show hypothesis → checked → ruled out → recommendation with the evidence package attached. *Traced to: UX-F20, UX-F24, UX-F25.*
2. **Make the chain-of-reasoning and data provenance visible and replayable.** A timeline mapping each agent step to the evidence and query that produced it. *Traced to: UX-F25, UX-F29.*
3. **Display confidence honestly with counter-evidence and blind spots** — never a lone clean accuracy %. Invite calibrated skepticism. *Traced to: UX-F30, UX-F43.*
4. **Gate every impactful/destructive action behind one-click approve/deny with policy exclusions.** Show a "Potential Response" panel flagging what needs approval. *Traced to: UX-F26, UX-F27.*
5. **Offer configurable autonomy tiers (in-the-loop / on-the-loop / auto-escalate)** scaled by action risk and routineness. *Traced to: UX-F28, UX-F16.*
6. **Role-adaptive explanation depth (Tier-1 concise, Tier-3 deep).** Progressive disclosure to avoid both blind faith and dismissal. *Traced to: UX-F21.*
7. **Immutable, queryable audit trail** recording every decision, reasoning chain, and which explanations were viewed before action. *Traced to: UX-F29.*
8. **Close the feedback loop: analyst corrections visibly retrain the system** and are acknowledged in-product. *Traced to: UX-F31, UX-F22.*
9. **Kill context-switching: unify evidence from all connectors into one investigation workspace** so the analyst never runs a "tab marathon." *Traced to: UX-F10, UX-F11, UX-F06.*
10. **Auto-draft compliant, plain-language incident reports on a regulatory clock** (CERT-In 6h / DPDP 72h), with a shift-handover summary that preserves hypothesis + evidence + time-sensitivity. *Traced to: UX-F34, UX-F41, UX-F42, UX-F32, UX-F33, UX-F40.*

### The 3 UX features that most impress a hackathon judge in a 30-second demo

**Feature A — The "Glass-Box" Investigation Replay.** In one screen, the agent swarm's reasoning plays back as a timeline: alert → triage agent verdict → correlation agent's linked events → threat-classification with MITRE ATT&CK mapping → knowledge agent's org-context → recommended response, each node clickable to the exact evidence/query. *Why it lands:* it directly answers the #1 real-world trust demand ("What did the AI do, and why?" — UX-F18, 90% say explainability is critical) and demonstrates chain-of-reasoning + provenance (UX-F25, UX-F29). Hits **User Experience** (evidence-first, calibrated trust) and **Clarity** (a judge instantly understands the whole system from one view). Peer-reviewed backing (UX-F20) makes it defensible against technical judges.

**Feature B — Policy-Guarded One-Click Response with a "Potential Impact" panel.** The demo shows a proposed containment (e.g., disable account / isolate host) with a red "requires approval" flag, the blast-radius/assets affected, an exclusion-policy note, and Approve/Deny — mirroring XSIAM's approach (UX-F27) and the 94% approval statistic. *Why it lands:* it neutralizes the single biggest fear (AI taking destructive actions at 2 a.m. — UX-F26, UX-F16) live, on stage. Hits **User Experience** (control + safety) and **Novelty** for a student build (policy-guarded autonomy tiers, UX-F28), and signals **Feasibility/Sustainability** (safe to deploy).

**Feature C — The Regulatory-Clock Auto-Report + Shift-Handover Card.** On confirming an incident, the system auto-generates a CERT-In-formatted 6-hour report draft and a DPDP-aware notification, plus a handover card (situation / actions taken / pending decisions with time-sensitivity). *Why it lands:* it is uniquely India-relevant and speaks directly to TCS/Wipro/Verizon judges who live the 6-hour mandate (UX-F34, UX-F41, UX-F42); it converts a documented time sink (UX-F01) and a documented failure mode (handover, UX-F32/F33) into an instant win. Hits **User Experience** (removes the worst toil), **Novelty** (no student demo typically nails Indian compliance), and **Scale of impact** (ties to ₹22,845 crore national loss context, UX-F45).

### Assessment of the proposed solution direction
The "domain-agnostic Autonomous Incident Intelligence Engine" (pluggable connectors → multi-agent swarm → policy-guarded response → auto playbooks, skinned as a Cyber SOC) is **well-aligned with evidence**, with three cautions:
- **Endorsed:** multi-agent specialization (triage/correlation/classification/response/knowledge) matches how leaders decompose the problem (Torq HyperAgents, D3 Morpheus), and policy-guarded response matches the universal approval-gate pattern (UX-F27). The connector-unification thesis directly attacks tool sprawl (UX-F11) and context-switching (UX-F10).
- **Caution 1 — trust UX is the differentiator, not the swarm.** Evidence shows judges/analysts reward transparency and calibrated trust over raw autonomy (UX-F18, UX-F20). Invest disproportionately in Features A–B.
- **Caution 2 — avoid "explanation theater."** Fluent explanations can *increase* over-reliance (UX-F43); include forcing-functions/counter-evidence (UX-F30) to show sophistication.
- **Caution 3 — "domain-agnostic" is a double-edged sword.** Depth-of-integration and org-context memory (UX-F31) are what make verdicts trustworthy; a thin generic layer risks shallow verdicts. For the demo, show deep context on ONE alert type (e.g., phishing or impossible-travel login) rather than shallow coverage of many. This is also the pragmatic path on free-tier infra (Render/n8n/Tavily + Claude): one deep, explainable, policy-guarded workflow beats a broad but shallow swarm.
